## Why this is an appendix

Studying a subject and preparing for an interview about it are different activities, and organising the first around the second produces a specific kind of damage: you learn which answer is expected rather than why it's right, and the knowledge doesn't transfer to a problem nobody wrote a guide for.

So the rest of this atlas is organised around understanding, in prerequisite order, with derivations and primary sources. This section is a **translation layer**: how that understanding maps onto a 45-minute conversation with a stranger holding a rubric.

If you never interview again, you lose nothing by skipping it. If you have a loop next month, it's the most immediately useful thing here.

## Using it without distorting your studying

**Read it early, then set it down.** Knowing what's being scored makes practice more efficient. Re-reading it instead of studying does not.

**Do not let it become the curriculum.** The failure mode is preparing exclusively for the eight questions that appear most often, which produces someone who can perform eight designs and cannot reason about a ninth. Good interviewers probe for exactly that, and it's also just a worse outcome — you took the time to study and came away with less than you could have.

**The genuinely transferable skill in here is communication.** Scoping an ambiguous problem, narrating a design while drawing it, defending a choice against a challenge, and admitting uncertainty precisely — those are real engineering skills that happen to also be what's scored. The parts that are purely about performing for a rubric are marked as such, and you can weight them accordingly.

:::note On the framing in these pages
These were written before the rest of the atlas was reorganised, and they still speak in the register of interview preparation — "senior signal", "what to say out loud", "traps". That register is appropriate here and nowhere else.

Where a page makes a technical claim, the underlying material is linked, and the linked version is the one that explains why it's true.
:::

## If you have three weeks

[Route D in the study plan](../00-orientation/study-plan.html) is the honest sprint: this appendix first, then eight case studies, backfilling concepts only when one exposes a gap, then two mock interviews with a real person.

It is the least effective way to use this material and the correct choice under a real deadline. Come back to the rest afterwards — it's more interesting when nothing depends on it.
