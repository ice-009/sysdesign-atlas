## The problem with reading about systems

You can read a well-written explanation of consistent hashing, follow every step, and close the tab convinced you understand it. Two weeks later, asked to design a cache tier, you will reach for `hash(key) % N` — because reading produced *familiarity*, and design requires *retrieval and application*.

This is not a discipline problem. It's a well-documented property of how learning works: the fluency you feel while reading a clear explanation is generated by the explanation, not by your own knowledge. The subjective sense of understanding is at its highest exactly when the least learning is happening.

Systems design makes this worse than most subjects, for two reasons:

1. **Every answer is "it depends."** There is no set of facts to memorise. The skill is judgment about tradeoffs under constraints, and judgment is only built by making calls and finding out.
2. **The feedback loop is broken.** A wrong architectural decision doesn't fail immediately. It fails eighteen months later at 40× the traffic, by which time you've moved teams. Most engineers get a handful of genuine feedback signals in a career.

The four habits below exist to attack these directly.

## Habit 1 — Estimate before you look

Before reading any page that involves a number, write down your guess.

*How much does a cache miss cost, relative to a hit? How many QPS can one Postgres instance handle? How much memory does a 4,000-token conversation consume on a GPU? What fraction of a fleet fails per day at 10,000 machines?*

Write the number down. Then read.

:::goat Why this works when re-reading doesn't
Committing to a wrong answer before seeing the right one produces dramatically better retention than reading the right one twice. The error creates a gap your brain then wants closed, and closing it is what forms the memory.

This is the single cheapest change you can make to how you read this atlas, and it costs about five seconds per number. Almost nobody does it, because being wrong on purpose feels unproductive — which is precisely why it works.
:::

The compounding benefit is that estimation becomes automatic. After a few dozen reps you stop needing the reference: you know a memory access is ~100 ns and a cross-continent round trip is ~70 ms and you can derive most of the space between them. [Back-of-envelope estimation](../01-foundations/back-of-envelope.html) is the explicit page on this, and [the hardware numbers](../01-foundations/hardware-numbers.html) is the source data — but the habit matters more than either.

## Habit 2 — Generate before you consume

For every case study and every build in this atlas: **spend twenty timeboxed minutes designing it yourself first.** Paper, whiteboard, a text file. Then read.

You will produce something worse than the written version. That's the point — the delta *is* the lesson, and it's specific to what you personally don't know rather than generic.

## Habit 3 — Trace one request all the way down

Take a single operation — "user posts a comment" — and follow it through every layer, out loud or in writing, without skipping.

DNS resolution. TLS handshake. Load balancer choosing a backend. Connection pool checkout. Request parsing. Auth token validation — is that a network call? Business logic. Database write: which index gets updated, does the write go to the WAL first, when is it fsynced, when does the replica see it? Cache invalidation — before or after the commit, and what happens in the window? Response serialisation. Bytes on the wire. The client render.

:::goat Where the real questions come from
Tracing is how you find out what you don't know. Every step where you say "and then it… gets saved" is a gap. The interesting questions surface automatically:

- If the cache is invalidated *before* the transaction commits, what does a concurrent reader see?
- If the client retries after a timeout, does the comment get posted twice? What makes it not?
- Which of these steps happens on every request versus once per connection?
- If the replica is 200 ms behind, does the user see their own comment after posting?

That last one is [read-your-writes consistency](../01-foundations/consistency-models.html), and discovering it by tracing is worth more than reading a definition of it.
:::

Do this once for a system you actually work on. It will be uncomfortable and it will produce a longer list of gaps than any curriculum.

## Habit 4 — Be specifically wrong

Vague understanding hides behind vague statements. "We'd add caching to improve performance" cannot be wrong, because it doesn't claim anything.

Force yourself into falsifiable claims:

| Vague | Falsifiable |
|---|---|
| "Add a cache" | "A 90% hit rate on a 200 ms query gets p50 to ~20 ms and cuts DB load 10×" |
| "It won't scale" | "At 5,000 writes/s, one Postgres primary saturates on WAL fsync" |
| "Use a queue" | "A queue decouples the 200/s producer from the 50/s consumer, at the cost of ~30 s of lag during peaks" |
| "Shard the database" | "Shard by user_id — but then the 'top posts globally' query has to scatter-gather across all shards" |

The right-hand claims can all be checked, and some will turn out wrong. That's the mechanism. A claim precise enough to be wrong is a claim you can learn from; a claim too vague to be wrong teaches nothing whether you keep it or drop it.

## What order to read in

The prerequisite graph handles the ordering — follow the readiness banners. Two things it doesn't tell you:

**Don't read a whole track before applying any of it.** Concepts decay quickly when unused. After each Building Blocks page, glance at a case study that uses it. After Foundations, do the estimation exercises. The interleaving matters more than the sequence.

**Revisit foundations after applying them.** [CAP](../01-foundations/cap-pacelc.html) read cold is a definition to memorise. Re-read after designing a multi-region system and it's a description of a problem you now recognise. The second reading is where the understanding actually lands, and it takes ten minutes.

:::trap Marking pages complete is not progress
The progress ring is a bookmark, not a measurement. It records what you've read, which is weakly correlated with what you can do.

If you want a real measure: pick a system at random from the [question bank](../10-appendix/question-bank.html), design it in 45 minutes with no reference material, and see where you stall. The stalls are the truth. Do it monthly.
:::

## A realistic pace

Depth beats coverage, consistently and by a lot.

- **One or two concept pages per session**, with the exercises actually done, beats six pages skimmed.
- **One case study per week**, designed first and then read, beats four read passively.
- **Twenty minutes of tracing a real system** beats an hour of reading about a hypothetical one.

Getting through Core Systems properly takes most people a couple of months at a few hours a week. That's the honest number. Going faster is possible and produces familiarity rather than skill — which is fine if familiarity is what you need, as long as you know that's the trade you're making.

:::exercise Start now
Before reading any further:

1. Write down your estimate for each of these — no looking:
   - Requests per second one modern server can handle for a simple JSON API
   - Latency of a same-datacenter network round trip
   - Rows in a Postgres table before you need to think about sharding
   - Fraction of a 10,000-machine fleet that fails on an average day
2. Pick a system you've worked on. Trace one request through it, writing each step. Mark every step where you're guessing.
3. Count the guesses. That number is your starting point, and it's more useful than any assessment.
:::

:::paper On learning, not systems
*Make It Stick* (Brown, Roediger & McDaniel, 2014) is the accessible summary of the research behind habits 1 and 2 — retrieval practice, generation, and why re-reading feels effective and isn't.

The specific effect underneath Habit 1 is documented in Richland, Kornell & Kao, *The Pretesting Effect* (2009): guessing before instruction improves retention even when — especially when — the guess is wrong.
:::

## What to carry forward

- **Fluency while reading is produced by the writing, not by your knowledge.** Distrust it.
- **Estimate before you look.** Five seconds per number, large effect.
- **Design before you read.** The delta is your actual curriculum.
- **Trace one request end to end.** Every "and then it gets saved" is a gap.
- **Make falsifiable claims.** A statement too vague to be wrong teaches nothing.
- **Interleave theory and application**, and revisit foundations after applying them.
